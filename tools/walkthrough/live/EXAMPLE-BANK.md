### TREND · Museum visitors 2010–20 (line, TREND)

**GOOD (C1)**

> The line graph illustrates how many people, in thousands, visited three museums in a European city over the ten-year period from 2010 to 2020.
>
> Overall, the Science Museum attracted steadily more visitors throughout the decade, whereas both the Art Gallery and the History Museum declined. The Science Museum overtook the Art Gallery around 2014 and finished the period as the most popular of the three, while the History Museum remained the least visited.
>
> In 2010, the Science Museum received 420 thousand visitors, the lowest figure apart from the History Museum. Its numbers then rose every two years, reaching 570 thousand in 2014 and 710 thousand in 2018, before peaking at 760 thousand in 2020, which was almost double the starting figure.
>
> By contrast, the Art Gallery, which had been the most visited museum in 2010 with 610 thousand visitors, fell gradually to 520 thousand by 2020, despite a slight recovery in 2016. The History Museum followed a similar downward path, dropping from 380 thousand to 260 thousand over the same period, with a brief rise to 300 thousand in 2018.

Verdict: TA 8 · CC 8 · LR 8 · GRA 8 → **8.0** (C1). 180 words, 4 ¶, overview with no values, both comparisons explicit. Ceiling: none.  
Every figure checked: 420/570/710/760; 610→520 with the 2016 recovery (560→570); 380→260 with the 2018 rise (290→300); crossover 2014; 760/420 = 1.8 "almost double". ✔

>
> In 2010, the Science Museum had 420 thousand visitors. This number rose to 570 thousand in 2014 and 710 thousand in 2018. In 2020 it reaches the highest point at 760 thousand visitors, which was almost two times higher than in 2010, maybe because science is more interesting for young people.
>
> On the other hand, the Art Gallery was the most popular in 2010 with 610 thousand visitors, but it fell to 560 thousand in 2014 and 520 thousand in 2020. The History Museum also went down from 380 thousand in 2010 to 260 thousand in 2020, with a small rise in 2018.

Verdict: TA 6 · CC 7 · LR 6 · GRA 6 → **6.5** (mean 6.25; B2). 168 words. **Ceiling: reason in a detail → TA ≤6** ("maybe because science is more interesting for young people").

### SEQUENCE · Recycling glass bottles (process, SEQUENCE)

**GOOD (C1)**

> The diagram illustrates the stages by which used glass bottles are collected and turned into new ones.
>
> Overall, this is a man-made, cyclical process with eight stages, beginning with the collection of empty bottles at a bottle bank and ending with new bottles being delivered to shops. The stages fall into two phases: the first four prepare the waste glass, while the last four create the new product.
>
> At the first stage, used bottles are deposited in a bottle bank, from where they are transported by lorry to a recycling plant. Once there, the bottles are washed with high-pressure water and then sorted by colour into clear, green and brown glass. The sorted glass is then crushed into small pieces known as cullet.
>
> In the second phase, the cullet is melted in a furnace at 600–800 °C, and new raw materials are added at this point. The molten glass is then moulded into new bottles, which are filled and delivered to shops, after which the cycle begins again when the empty bottles are returned to the bottle bank.

Verdict: TA 8 · CC 8 · LR 8 · GRA 8 → **8.0** (C1). 178 words; passive throughout; 8/8 stages. Ceiling: none.  
Checked: eight stages, cyclical, 600–800 °C, three colours. ✔ One CC lapse: the overview says "the first four prepare the waste glass" but crushing (stage 5) sits in the phase-one paragraph.

**WEAK (B1, verbatim)**

> The diagram shows how glass bottles are recycled in eight steps.
>

Verdict: TA 6 · CC 7 · LR 6 · GRA 6 → **6.5** (mean 6.25; B2). 168 words. **Ceiling: opinion/reason → TA ≤6** ("This is a very good system because it saves energy").
