IELTS ACADEMIC WRITING TASK 1 · MODULE 12: COHESION
Listeners: Thai high school students, CEFR A2 to B2, aiming for Writing Band 7 to 8
Topic: pointing back instead of repeating, linking with meaning instead of counting,

HOW TO PRESENT THIS
================================================================
Two people talking the topic through with each other. The whole episode must be
UNDER SIX MINUTES. No welcome, no introductions, no "today we are going to talk
about", no small talk: the first sentence is the first fact below. Speak slowly and
clearly, in short sentences and everyday CEFR B1 words. Explain every IELTS term in
plain words the first time it is used. Say each example sentence slowly enough to be
written down. Use only the examples given here. Never mention notes, a document, a
source, a file, an app or where the information came from. Never speak to a
listener: no "you", no audience, no goodbye. Cover every point below, in order; if
time is short, shorten the chat around a point, never drop the point. Stop after the
last idea. Say numbers slowly and in full (twenty-five percent, not 25 percent), and
when an example describes a chart, describe the chart in words first.

1. POINT BACK INSTEAD OF REPEATING (about 1 minute 45)
================================================================

Cohesion means the links that join sentences together. It belongs to the score
called Coherence and Cohesion. A Band 6 report often repeats the same name in every
sentence. On a line graph of visitors to three museums in a European city from 2010
to 2020, the Art Gallery falls from six hundred and ten thousand visitors to five
hundred and twenty thousand. The weak version: "The Art Gallery began at six hundred
and ten thousand, but the Art Gallery fell to five hundred and twenty thousand." The
better version says "but it fell".

The Band 7 descriptor asks for "reference and substitution" used "flexibly".
Reference simply means pointing back to something already named instead of naming it
again. Four tools cover almost every case.

One: "it", "its" and "this figure". "The Science Museum received four hundred and
twenty thousand visitors in 2010, and this figure had risen to seven hundred and
sixty thousand by 2020." "This figure" points back to the number, not the museum.

Two: "the former" and "the latter", for the first and the second of two things just
named. Take a table of teachers in five countries. Korea and Japan have similar
starting salaries, but Korean teachers work forty-three hours a week and Japanese
teachers fifty-four. So: "Korea and Japan had similar starting salaries; however,
the former worked forty-three hours a week and the latter fifty-four." The order of
naming decides: Korea first, so Korea is the former.

Three: "both" and "the two", for a pair. Four: "respectively", which matches two
figures to two names in the same order: "Korea and Japan started on thirty-three
thousand and thirty-one thousand dollars respectively."

Time can be referred to as well: "over the same period". A good target is two
reference tools per report, each pointing clearly at one thing. A reference that
could mean two things is worse than repeating the name.

================================================================
2. FEWER CONNECTORS, BETTER LINKS (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

More linking words do not mean better cohesion. The Band 6 descriptor says cohesion
may be "faulty or mechanical due to misuse, overuse or omission". The classic
example is a paragraph that goes Firstly, Secondly, Thirdly, Finally. Picture two
pie charts of the sources of electricity in one country in 2000 and 2020. Coal falls
from forty-eight percent to eighteen percent, natural gas rises from twenty-two
percent to thirty percent, and wind and solar jump from five percent to twenty-seven
percent. "Firstly, coal fell. Secondly, gas rose. Thirdly, wind and solar rose."
Those words count sentences; they do not link ideas.

Good links carry meaning. For contrast: in contrast, by contrast, however, whereas,
while. For time: the following year, over the next decade, by 2020. So: "Coal fell
from forty-eight percent to eighteen percent. In contrast, natural gas rose from
twenty-two percent to thirty percent over the same period."

A connector must name the right relationship. "Moreover" adds a similar point, so it
cannot introduce a fall after a rise. "As a result" claims a cause, and a chart
never shows causes. Often a time phrase does the work alone. On a bar chart of
tourist arrivals in Thailand: "Arrivals peaked at thirty-nine point nine million in
2019. The following year, however, the figure collapsed to six point seven million."
When "however" opens a sentence, a comma follows it; between two full sentences it
needs a full stop or a semicolon before it.

================================================================
3. JOIN TWO SENTENCES WITH A CLAUSE (about 1 minute 45)
================================================================

Band 7 in the grammar score needs "a variety of complex structures", meaning
sentences with more than one clause. The easiest way is to join two simple sentences
about the same subject.

The first shape is the relative clause with "which". On the museum graph, the
Science Museum passes the Art Gallery in 2014 and reaches seven hundred and sixty
thousand in 2020. Two sentences become one: "The Science Museum, which overtook the
Art Gallery in 2014, reached seven hundred and sixty thousand visitors in 2020."
This extra-information clause takes a comma on both sides and never uses "that".
"The Science Museum, that overtook the Art Gallery" is wrong.

The second shape is the participle clause. It drops the subject and uses "having"
plus a past participle, or an -ing form. Picture a line graph of three city
populations from 1990, with projections to 2040. Osaka peaks at nineteen point three
million in 2010 and is projected to fall to seventeen point eight million by 2040.
So: "Having peaked at nineteen point three million in 2010, Osaka is projected to
fall to seventeen point eight million by 2040." The subject of both parts must be
the same: Osaka peaked, and Osaka is projected to fall.

The third shape is "with" plus a noun plus an -ing form. On a line graph of
households with internet access in four countries from 2005 to 2025, Thailand climbs
from twelve percent to eighty-eight percent, the biggest rise of the four. So:
"Internet access rose in all four countries, with Thailand showing the biggest
rise."

One relative clause and one participle clause per paragraph is plenty. The Band 6
descriptor warns that complex structures are often "not marked by the same level of
accuracy as in simple structures". So a faulty complex sentence scores below a
correct simple one.

================================================================
THE THING WORTH REMEMBERING
================================================================

Good cohesion is mostly invisible: point back with this figure, the former and
respectively, link with words that name a real contrast or a time, and drop the
counting words. Then join a few sentences with which, having, or with, and check
every comma.
