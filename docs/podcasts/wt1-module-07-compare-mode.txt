QUILLMOOR OBSERVATORY — MODULE 07 PODCAST BRIEFING
IELTS ACADEMIC WRITING TASK 1 · MODULE 07: COMPARE MODE
Listeners: Thai high school students, CEFR A2 to B2, aiming for Writing Band 7 to 8
Topic: comparing categories when nothing changes over time: bar charts without years,
single pie charts, and tables

================================================================
HOW TO PRESENT THIS
================================================================
Two people talking the topic through with each other. The whole episode must be
UNDER SIX MINUTES. No welcome, no introductions, no "today we are going to talk
about", no small talk: the first sentence is the first fact below. Speak slowly and
clearly, in short sentences and everyday CEFR B1 words. Explain every IELTS term in
plain words the first time it is used. Say each example sentence slowly enough to be
written down. Use only the examples given here. Never mention notes, a document, a
source, a file, an app or where the information came from. Never speak to a
listener: no "you", no audience, no goodbye. Cover every point below, in order; if
time is short, shorten the chat around a point, never drop the point. Stop after the
last idea. Say numbers slowly and in full (twenty-five percent, not 25 percent), and
when an example describes a chart, describe the chart in words first.

================================================================
1. BAR CHARTS: HIGHER, HIGHEST, TWICE AS (about 1 minute 45)
================================================================

COMPARE mode is for charts with no time axis, only categories, so nothing rises or
falls.

A bar chart shows weekly hours that four age groups spent on three activities in
2023. Social media was 21 hours for ages 13 to 17, then 18, 11, and 6 for people aged
50 and over. Streaming was 12 to 15 hours in every group. Gaming went from 12 hours
for teenagers to 1 hour for the over-50s.

The classic mistake: "Gaming fell sharply with age." "Fell" is a trend verb. The fix:
"Gaming was far lower among older people."

Three tools do the work: comparatives (higher than), superlatives (the highest) and
multiples (four times as much as).

Pattern S5 is the comparative with both figures: "Social media use was far higher
among people aged 13 to 17 than among those aged 50 and over, at 21 and 6 hours a
week respectively." "Respectively" means "in the same order"; it goes at the end,
with three names at most.

Pattern S6 is the multiple: "Teenagers spent four times as much time gaming as people
aged 30 to 49." Twelve hours against three; the arithmetic must be right.

Near-equal bars: "Streaming was roughly equal across the four age groups, at between
12 and 15 hours." The chart is dated 2023, so the verbs are past simple, and a noun
after a number above one is plural: five hours.

================================================================
2. PIE CHARTS: SHARES AND FRACTIONS (about 1 minute 15)
================================================================

A single pie shows shares of one hundred percent. Its verbs: accounted for, made up,
represented.

A pie chart shows a secondary school student's weekly spending in 2024. Food and
drinks 38 percent, transport 22, phone and internet 15, clothes 12, entertainment 9,
and savings 4.

The error: "Savings rose to 4%." One pie, one year. The fix: "Savings represented
just 4%."

Fractions add range, always paired with the exact figure: "Food and drinks accounted
for the largest share, at 38%, well over a third of the total."

Big slices can be combined: "Together, food and transport made up 60% of the budget,
while the other four categories shared the remaining 40%."

But two pie charts for two different years need trend verbs for the change.

================================================================
3. TABLES: WHOLE ROWS AND COLUMNS (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

In a table every number looks equally important. It is not.

A table shows teachers in five countries in 2022: starting salary, salary after 15
years, and weekly hours. Germany: 62,000 dollars, 81,000, 40 hours. The USA: 44,000,
66,000, 46. Korea: 33,000, 58,000, 43. Japan: 31,000, 55,000, 54. Thailand: 9,000,
17,000, 45.

Read down each column and along each row: the biggest and smallest numbers are the
key features.

Compare whole columns or rows, not single cells: "Starting salaries ranged from
$9,000 in Thailand to $62,000 in Germany, almost seven times as much." Or two columns
of one row: "Japanese teachers worked the longest hours, at 54 a week, whereas their
starting salary was among the lowest, at $31,000."

The list that loses marks: "Korea's starting salary was $33,000. Korea's salary after
15 years was $58,000." Nothing is compared.

If the columns are years, the table is TREND. If they are measures, like salary and
hours, it is COMPARE.

================================================================
THE THING WORTH REMEMBERING
================================================================

No time axis means no rising and no falling. Rank the categories, give the gap as a
multiple or a fraction, and always compare two things in the same sentence.
