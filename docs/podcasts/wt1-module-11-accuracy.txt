QUILLMOOR OBSERVATORY — MODULE 11 PODCAST BRIEFING
IELTS ACADEMIC WRITING TASK 1 · MODULE 11: ACCURACY
Listeners: Thai high school students, CEFR A2 to B2, aiming for Writing Band 7 to 8
Topic: the seven error families, E1 to E7, that stop sentences being error-free, and
a three-pass check to find them

================================================================
HOW TO PRESENT THIS
================================================================
Two people talking the topic through with each other. The whole episode must be
UNDER SIX MINUTES. No welcome, no introductions, no "today we are going to talk
about", no small talk: the first sentence is the first fact below. Speak slowly and
clearly, in short sentences and everyday CEFR B1 words. Explain every IELTS term in
plain words the first time it is used. Say each example sentence slowly enough to be
written down. Use only the examples given here. Never mention notes, a document, a
source, a file, an app or where the information came from. Never speak to a
listener: no "you", no audience, no goodbye. Cover every point below, in order; if
time is short, shorten the chat around a point, never drop the point. Stop after the
last idea. Say numbers slowly and in full (twenty-five percent, not 25 percent), and
when an example describes a chart, describe the chart in words first.

================================================================
1. ARTICLES, PLURALS, NUMBER AND AMOUNT (about 1 minute 45)
================================================================

Grammatical Range and Accuracy is the grammar score. Band 7 needs "error-free
sentences are frequent", which means more than half of the sentences have no mistake
at all. Band 8 needs "The majority of sentences are error-free", so about three in
four. Most of the errors that block this are small and repeated. So the smart move
is to fix families of errors, not single mistakes. There are seven families, called
E1 to E7: articles, plurals and countability, subject-verb agreement, tense,
prepositions with figures, punctuation, and finally word form, collocation and
spelling.

E1 is articles: a, an and the. The fixed phrases of a report always take "the": the
line graph, the number of, the proportion of, the highest. Take a line graph of
visitors to three museums in a European city from 2010 to 2020. The Science Museum
rises from four hundred and twenty thousand visitors to seven hundred and sixty
thousand. The wrong sentence begins "Number of visitors to the Science Museum rose."
The right one is: "The number of visitors to the Science Museum rose from four
hundred and twenty thousand to seven hundred and sixty thousand." A change named for
the first time takes "a": "There was a strong rise in visitors to the Science
Museum."

E2 is plurals and countability. After a number bigger than one, the noun needs an s:
three countries, three museums. Thai has no plural ending, so this needs a
deliberate check on every noun after a number. A table of teachers' working hours in
five countries shows Germany at forty hours a week, Korea at forty-three, Thailand
at forty-five, the USA at forty-six and Japan at fifty-four. So: "Teachers in three
of the five countries worked between forty-three and forty-six hours a week." The
same family covers "number" and "amount". "The number of" goes with things that can
be counted: visitors, cars, tourists. "The amount of" goes with things that cannot:
money, electricity, time. "The amount of tourists" is wrong; it is "the number of
tourists". "The proportion of" works with both.

================================================================
2. AGREEMENT, PUNCTUATION AND SPELLING (about 1 minute 45)
================================================================

E3 is subject-verb agreement. The trick is to find the head noun, the main noun of
the subject. In "the number of visitors", the head noun is "number", which is
singular. So: "The number of visitors to the History Museum was two hundred and
sixty thousand in 2020", not "were". In "the figures for China and India", the head
noun is "figures", so the verb is plural: "were".

E6 is punctuation. A comma follows an opening phrase: "In 2020, ..." and "Overall,
...". Two full sentences can never be joined by a comma alone. That error is called
a comma splice. Picture two pie charts of the sources of electricity in one country
in 2000 and 2020. Coal falls from forty-eight percent to eighteen percent, and
natural gas rises from twenty-two percent to thirty percent. "Coal fell to eighteen
percent, natural gas rose to thirty percent" is a comma splice. The fix: "Coal fell
to eighteen percent in 2020, while natural gas rose to thirty percent." Apostrophes
belong to this family too: "the student's weekly spending".

E7 is word form, collocation and spelling. Word form: "a sharply increase" should be
"a sharp increase", because an adjective goes before a noun. Spelling: about ten
words carry most reports, and they must be exact: percentage, significantly,
approximately, fluctuated, decreased, proportion, whereas, respectively, which and
throughout. "Percent" goes after a number, as in twenty-five percent; "percentage"
never does.

================================================================
3. NAME THE FAMILY, THEN FIX IT (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

Two families need a final reminder. E4 is tense, and tense follows the dates. Past
years take the past simple; a process or a chart with no dates takes the present;
future years take forms like "is expected to". A bar chart of international tourist
arrivals in Thailand shows a peak of thirty-nine point nine million in 2019.
"Arrivals increase to thirty-nine point nine million in 2019" is wrong. 2019 is in
the past, so it is "increased".

E5 is prepositions with figures. "Rose to" gives the end point. "Rose by" gives the
size of the change. "From ... to" gives both, and a level takes "at": "peaked at",
"stood at". On the museum graph, visits to the Art Gallery fall from six hundred and
ten thousand to five hundred and twenty thousand. "Fell to ninety thousand" is
wrong, because ninety thousand is the size of the fall, not the end point. The right
sentence: "Visits to the Art Gallery fell by ninety thousand, from six hundred and
ten thousand to five hundred and twenty thousand." With percentages, careful writers
say "percentage points": coal fell by thirty percentage points, from forty-eight
percent to eighteen percent.

A student who can name the family finds the error faster. A student who keeps a log
of her own families stops repeating them. The check takes about three minutes and
uses three quick passes. First pass, the verbs: tense and agreement. Second pass,
the nouns: articles, plurals, number or amount. Third pass, the figures:
prepositions and commas. Three quick passes find more errors than one slow read.

================================================================
THE THING WORTH REMEMBERING
================================================================

Band 7 grammar is not only about clever sentences; above all, it needs many
sentences with no errors. Seven families, E1 to E7, cause most of the errors, and
three quick passes through verbs, nouns and figures catch most of them.
