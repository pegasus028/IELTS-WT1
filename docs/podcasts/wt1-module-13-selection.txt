QUILLMOOR OBSERVATORY — MODULE 13 PODCAST BRIEFING
IELTS ACADEMIC WRITING TASK 1 · MODULE 13: SELECTION
Listeners: Thai high school students, CEFR A2 to B2, aiming for Writing Band 7 to 8
Topic: what a key feature is, choosing three or four before writing, and proving
each one with a few well-chosen figures instead of listing them all

================================================================
HOW TO PRESENT THIS
================================================================
Two people talking the topic through with each other. The whole episode must be
UNDER SIX MINUTES. No welcome, no introductions, no "today we are going to talk
about", no small talk: the first sentence is the first fact below. Speak slowly and
clearly, in short sentences and everyday CEFR B1 words. Explain every IELTS term in
plain words the first time it is used. Say each example sentence slowly enough to be
written down. Use only the examples given here. Never mention notes, a document, a
source, a file, an app or where the information came from. Never speak to a
listener: no "you", no audience, no goodbye. Cover every point below, in order; if
time is short, shorten the chat around a point, never drop the point. Stop after the
last idea. Say numbers slowly and in full (twenty-five percent, not 25 percent), and
when an example describes a chart, describe the chart in words first.

================================================================
1. WHAT IS A KEY FEATURE? (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

Every Task 1 question asks for "selecting and reporting the main features", which is
marked under Task Achievement. Band 7 asks for key features that are "clearly
highlighted". Band 8 says "Key features are skilfully selected". So what counts as a
key feature?

A key feature is a general truth about the whole chart, the kind of thing a
newspaper would report. Here is the example for this topic: a line graph of visitors
to three museums in a European city from 2010 to 2020. The Science Museum climbs
from four hundred and twenty thousand visitors to seven hundred and sixty thousand.
The Art Gallery falls from six hundred and ten thousand to five hundred and twenty
thousand. The History Museum falls from three hundred and eighty thousand to two
hundred and sixty thousand, and it is the lowest line in every year. In 2014 the
Science Museum passes the Art Gallery.

Key features come in five kinds. The direction: the Science Museum rose strongly,
while the other two declined. The largest and smallest: the History Museum was the
least visited throughout. The biggest change or gap. A crossover, where two lines
change places: the Science Museum overtook the Art Gallery in 2014. And an
exception, one item that moves against the rest. On a table of rice production in
four countries in 2000, 2010 and 2020, China, India and Vietnam all rise after 2010,
but Thailand falls from thirty-six million tonnes to thirty million. Thailand is the
exception.

What is not a key feature: one single figure, such as "The Art Gallery had five
hundred and ninety thousand visitors in 2012". A count, such as "three museums are
shown". A small wobble, such as the History Museum's slight recovery in 2018. And
any reason or opinion. A single figure is evidence for a feature; it is not a
feature.

Two more tests. A key feature must say something: "There were some differences
between the museums" is too vague. And it must be true: "The Art Gallery was the
most visited throughout" sounds like a feature, but it is false from 2014 onwards.

================================================================
2. CHOOSE FOUR BEFORE WRITING (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

Every chart has more facts than a report of about one hundred and seventy words can
hold. The strong writer chooses three or four key features and leaves the rest out
on purpose. They usually come from the five kinds above. Two of them go into the
overview, without figures. All of them get evidence in the detail paragraphs.

The choice is made visible before the first sentence: four short notes in the margin
of the question paper. For the museum graph: "Science up. Art and History down.
Crossover 2014. History lowest throughout." These notes are only for planning. The
report itself is always full sentences in paragraphs, never notes or bullet points.
If a fact does not fit under one of the four notes, it does not go in.

The danger is the fact that is easy to write but earns nothing: a true fact that is
too small, a false claim, a count, or a reason. Take a bar chart of international
tourist arrivals in Thailand from 2015 to 2025: a rise to a peak in 2019, a collapse
in 2020 and 2021, then a strong recovery. Three phases, three key features. "The
fall in 2020 was because of the pandemic" is not one of them. The cause is not on
the chart, and in a Task 1 report it is irrelevant material.

================================================================
3. ILLUSTRATE, DON'T LIST (about 2 minutes)
================================================================

Band 8 says key features are "highlighted and illustrated". Band 7 says they "could
be more fully or more appropriately illustrated". So the step from 7 to 8 is not
more figures; it is better-chosen figures. To illustrate a feature is to give the
one or two figures that prove it. For a rise, the start and the end. For a gap, the
two extremes. For a crossover, the year the lines change places.

An illustrated sentence: "The Science Museum climbed from four hundred and twenty
thousand visitors in 2010 to seven hundred and sixty thousand in 2020, passing the
Art Gallery in 2014." Two features, three figures.

A listed sentence: "In 2010 it had four hundred and twenty thousand, in 2012 four
hundred and seventy thousand, in 2014 five hundred and seventy thousand, and in 2016
six hundred and thirty thousand." Four figures and no feature. The Band 5 descriptor
calls this kind of reporting "mainly mechanical".

A gap can be illustrated with its two ends and a fraction. On a table of teachers'
starting salaries in five countries, Thailand is lowest at nine thousand US dollars
and Germany highest at sixty-two thousand. So: "Thailand's starting salary of nine
thousand dollars was less than a sixth of Germany's sixty-two thousand."

Accuracy still rules, because an illustration only works if the figure is right. On
the tourism chart, arrivals reach thirty-six point five million in 2025, against the
2019 peak of thirty-nine point nine million. "By 2025, arrivals had recovered to
thirty-six point five million, close to the 2019 peak" is correct. "Slightly above
the 2019 peak" is false. When a value falls between two gridlines, words like
"around" or "just under" keep it honest. An exact figure that is clearly wrong
counts as a factual error.

================================================================
THE THING WORTH REMEMBERING
================================================================

A Band 8 report does not say everything. It chooses three or four key features
before writing, puts two of them in the overview, and proves each one with the few
figures that make it visible.
