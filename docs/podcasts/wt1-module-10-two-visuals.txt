QUILLMOOR OBSERVATORY — MODULE 10 PODCAST BRIEFING
IELTS ACADEMIC WRITING TASK 1 · MODULE 10: TWO VISUALS
Listeners: Thai high school students, CEFR A2 to B2, aiming for Writing Band 7 to 8
Topic: two visuals in one task: one introduction that names both, one overview with
a headline for each, and one detail paragraph for each visual

================================================================
HOW TO PRESENT THIS
================================================================
Two people talking the topic through with each other. The whole episode must be
UNDER SIX MINUTES. No welcome, no introductions, no "today we are going to talk
about", no small talk: the first sentence is the first fact below. Speak slowly and
clearly, in short sentences and everyday CEFR B1 words. Explain every IELTS term in
plain words the first time it is used. Say each example sentence slowly enough to be
written down. Use only the examples given here. Never mention notes, a document, a
source, a file, an app or where the information came from. Never speak to a
listener: no "you", no audience, no goodbye. Cover every point below, in order; if
time is short, shorten the chat around a point, never drop the point. Stop after the
last idea. Say numbers slowly and in full (twenty-five percent, not 25 percent), and
when an example describes a chart, describe the chart in words first.

================================================================
1. ONE INTRODUCTION FOR TWO VISUALS (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

Some Task 1 questions show two visuals at once: a bar chart and a table, or a line
graph and a pie chart. Many students panic and write two separate reports. That is a
mistake. The report still has four paragraphs: an introduction, an overview, and two
detail paragraphs. Only two things change. The introduction names both visuals, and
each detail paragraph takes one visual.

Here is the example for this whole topic. A bar chart shows the average number of
cups of coffee one person drank per week in five countries in 2024. Finland is at
twenty-one cups, Italy fourteen, Brazil twelve, Japan eight and Thailand five. Next
to it, a table gives the average price of one cup in the same five countries, in US
dollars. Finland is three dollars ninety, Japan three dollars sixty, Thailand two
dollars twenty, Italy one dollar forty and Brazil one dollar ten.

Before writing, each visual gets its own quick check: what type it is, what it
measures, and the units. Here the bar chart counts cups and the table gives prices.

The introduction is one sentence with two halves: "The bar chart shows how many cups
of coffee people drank per week in five countries in 2024, while the table gives the
average price of a cup in each country." The words "each country" point back to the
five countries already named, so the list is not repeated. Small linking phrases
like "in each country", "for the same five countries" or "over the same period" show
the examiner that the writer has seen how the two visuals connect.

The weak version is two short sentences: "The bar chart shows coffee consumption.
The table shows coffee prices." They are not connected, and they give no place, no
year and no units. "While" joins two visuals that sit side by side, and "and" works
too. "Because" or "so" would be wrong, since the visuals show no causes.

================================================================
2. ONE OVERVIEW, TWO HEADLINES (about 1 minute 45)
================================================================

The overview is the paragraph that sums up the main features. With two visuals it
still comes second, still begins with "Overall," and still has no data values. The
difference is that it gives one headline for each visual: "Overall, Finland consumed
by far the most coffee and Thailand the least, while prices were highest in Finland
and Japan and lowest in Brazil and Italy." One headline for the chart, one for the
table, no numbers.

If the same categories appear in both visuals, the writer looks for a link and
states it in one general sentence: "Finland was the leader on both measures, whereas
Italy and Brazil combined high consumption with low prices." That is true. Finland
is first for cups and first for price, while Italy and Brazil are second and third
for cups but have the two lowest prices. A true link like this helps the paragraph
become what the Band 7 descriptor calls "a clear overview".

There are three traps. The first is writing one overview per visual, which turns one
answer into two small reports. The second is an overview that covers only one visual
and forgets the other. The third is inventing a link the data do not support. "The
more coffee people drink, the cheaper it is" sounds clever, but it is false, because
Finland drinks the most and also pays the most. A link must be checked against every
country before it is written.

================================================================
3. ONE PARAGRAPH FOR EACH VISUAL (about 1 minute 45)
================================================================

The detail paragraphs are the easy part, because the grouping is already done: one
visual, one paragraph. Paragraph three reports the bar chart. Paragraph four reports
the table. Each starts with a short topic sentence that says what it is about, and
each has its own figures and at least one comparison.

Paragraph three, cups only: "Finland was far ahead, at twenty-one cups per person
per week, while Italy and Brazil followed with fourteen and twelve cups
respectively." The word "respectively" matches the two figures to the two countries
in the same order.

Paragraph four, prices only: "A cup in Finland cost three dollars ninety, more than
three times the Brazilian price of one dollar ten."

The rule is: never mix the visuals. "Brazil, at twelve cups, also paid the lowest
price of one dollar ten" puts a cups figure and a price figure in one sentence, so
it belongs to neither paragraph. Figures from the two visuals are also never added
together or turned into one calculation. A short reference back is fine, though:
"Italy and Brazil, the two big consumers after Finland, paid the least." That
sentence sits in the price paragraph and points back to the bar chart without
borrowing its numbers.

Selection works exactly as with one visual: the highest, the lowest, one comparison,
one interesting pair. Five countries do not need five sentences. Going down the list
one country at a time is what the descriptors call "mechanical". And if one visual
has years and the other does not, the verbs change at the paragraph break: past
tense and trend verbs such as "rose" for a line graph from 2000 to 2020, then
comparison language with no trend verbs for a table of one year.

================================================================
THE THING WORTH REMEMBERING
================================================================

Two visuals are still one report: one introduction that names both, one overview
with a headline for each, and one paragraph for each visual with its own figures.
The visuals are linked with words like "each country", never with mixed numbers.
