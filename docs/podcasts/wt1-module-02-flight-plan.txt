QUILLMOOR OBSERVATORY — MODULE 02 PODCAST BRIEFING
IELTS ACADEMIC WRITING TASK 1 · MODULE 02: FLIGHT PLAN
Listeners: Thai high school students, CEFR A2 to B2, aiming for Writing Band 7 to 8
Topic: the four-paragraph skeleton, the introduction pattern, and paraphrasing the
task sentence instead of copying it

================================================================
HOW TO PRESENT THIS
================================================================
Two people talking the topic through with each other. The whole episode must be
UNDER SIX MINUTES. No welcome, no introductions, no "today we are going to talk
about", no small talk: the first sentence is the first fact below. Speak slowly and
clearly, in short sentences and everyday CEFR B1 words. Explain every IELTS term in
plain words the first time it is used. Say each example sentence slowly enough to be
written down. Use only the examples given here. Never mention notes, a document, a
source, a file, an app or where the information came from. Never speak to a
listener: no "you", no audience, no goodbye. Cover every point below, in order; if
time is short, shorten the chat around a point, never drop the point. Stop after the
last idea. Say numbers slowly and in full (twenty-five percent, not 25 percent), and
when an example describes a chart, describe the chart in words first.

================================================================
1. FOUR PARAGRAPHS, ONE JOB EACH (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

Every Task 1 report, whatever the chart, uses the same skeleton, called the flight
plan. Paragraph 1, the introduction: what the chart shows, where and when. Paragraph
2, the overview: it begins "Overall," and gives the biggest general truths, with no
data values. Paragraphs 3 and 4, the details: one group of features each, and every
sentence carries a figure.

The rough sizes are thirty words, forty-five words, sixty words and sixty words. The
introduction is the smallest; the details are the largest, because that is where the
evidence is. Together they land near the target of one hundred and sixty to one
hundred and ninety words.

The overview goes second, never last, so it is on the page even if time runs out.

There is no fifth paragraph. Task 1 has no conclusion. The report ends with its last
detail sentence.

Here is the skeleton on one chart: a line graph of visitors to three museums in a
European city from 2010 to 2020. The Science Museum rose; the Art Gallery and the
History Museum fell. Paragraph 1: "The line graph illustrates how many people visited
three museums in a European city from 2010 to 2020." Paragraph 2: "Overall, the
Science Museum grew strongly over the decade, whereas the other two museums lost
visitors." Paragraph 3: "The Science Museum rose from four hundred and twenty thousand
visitors in 2010 to seven hundred and sixty thousand in 2020." Paragraph 4 does the
same for the other two museums.

Paragraphs matter for Coherence and Cohesion. A report written as one block of text
cannot score above Band 5 for that criterion. Two paragraphs cannot go above Band 6.
A common shape is a short introduction, a short overview, then one long block of
details. The fix is to split the details into two groups, one paragraph each.

================================================================
2. THE INTRODUCTION PATTERN (about 1 minute 45)
================================================================

The introduction is one sentence built from a pattern: The [visual] shows [what is
measured] in [where] between [first year] and [last year]. Every slot is filled from
the chart itself: the title, the axis labels and the legend. If the chart has no
dates, the time slot goes.

The visual word must be right: line graph, bar chart, pie chart, table, diagram, map.
The "what" needs the right noun. "The number of" is for things that can be counted:
visitors, tourists, cars. "The amount of" is for things that cannot: electricity,
money, rice. "The percentage of" or "the proportion of" is for a chart in percent.

A line graph shows the share of homes with the internet in Sweden, Japan, Thailand
and Kenya from 2005 to 2025. "The line graph shows the percentage of households with
internet access in four countries between 2005 and 2025." Every slot is filled.

The units belong in the introduction too, because no figure later makes sense without
them. A table shows rice production in four Asian countries in 2000, 2010 and 2020.
"The table compares rice production in four Asian countries in 2000, 2010 and 2020.
The figures are in million tonnes."

Articles are part of the pattern: "the line graph", "the number of". Most country
names take no article, so "in Thailand", but there are fixed ones: the UK, the USA.
"Electricity" in general takes no article either.

And no findings. "The bar chart shows that teenagers spent the most time on social
media" puts a result in paragraph 1. Results belong in the overview.

================================================================
3. PARAPHRASE, DON'T COPY (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

The descriptors say: "Any copied rubric must be discounted." Words copied from the
task sentence are removed from the word count and are not counted as the writer's
English. So a report of one hundred and fifty words with a twenty-word copied
introduction is really a report of one hundred and thirty. As a working rule, a run
of ten identical words in a row counts as copied, and eight or nine is too close.

The introduction is a paraphrase: the same facts in new words. Three swaps are
enough. The verb: shows becomes illustrates, compares, or gives information about.
The noun phrase: "the number of visitors" becomes "how many people visited"; "the
sources of electricity" becomes "how electricity was generated". The time phrase:
"between 2010 and 2020" becomes "from 2010 to 2020", or "over a ten-year period".

The museum task sentence says: "The line graph shows the number of visitors, in
thousands, to three museums in a European city between 2010 and 2020." The
paraphrase: "The line graph illustrates how many people visited three museums in a
European city over a ten-year period from 2010 to 2020. The figures are given in
thousands." Same facts, new shape.

A paraphrase must keep the meaning. "In a European city" cannot become "in Europe";
one city is not a continent. "The number of visitors" cannot become "the amount of
visitors". "Shows" cannot become "proves". Some words never change: 2010 stays 2010,
Thailand stays Thailand, coal stays coal.

The verb must also be formal. "The line graph talks about" is spoken English, and a
chart cannot talk. Shows, illustrates, compares, presents.

================================================================
THE THING WORTH REMEMBERING
================================================================

Introduction, overview, detail, detail, and then stop. The introduction fills every
slot from the chart and says it in the writer's own words, because copied words are
not counted.
