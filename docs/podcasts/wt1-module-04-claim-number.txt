QUILLMOOR OBSERVATORY — MODULE 04 PODCAST BRIEFING
IELTS ACADEMIC WRITING TASK 1 · MODULE 04: CLAIM + NUMBER
Listeners: Thai high school students, CEFR A2 to B2, aiming for Writing Band 7 to 8
Topic: a figure in every detail sentence, the right preposition with every figure,
and safe approximation with the right unit

================================================================
HOW TO PRESENT THIS
================================================================
Two people talking the topic through with each other. The whole episode must be
UNDER SIX MINUTES. No welcome, no introductions, no "today we are going to talk
about", no small talk: the first sentence is the first fact below. Speak slowly and
clearly, in short sentences and everyday CEFR B1 words. Explain every IELTS term in
plain words the first time it is used. Say each example sentence slowly enough to be
written down. Use only the examples given here. Never mention notes, a document, a
source, a file, an app or where the information came from. Never speak to a
listener: no "you", no audience, no goodbye. Cover every point below, in order; if
time is short, shorten the chat around a point, never drop the point. Stop after the
last idea. Say numbers slowly and in full (twenty-five percent, not 25 percent), and
when an example describes a chart, describe the chart in words first.

================================================================
1. EVERY DETAIL SENTENCE CARRIES A FIGURE (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

Paragraphs 3 and 4 are the detail paragraphs. The Task Achievement descriptors draw a
sharp line here. Band 5: "There may be no data to support the description." Band 6:
details are "supported using figures/data". The difference is one habit: every
sentence in the detail paragraphs carries a figure, a date or a label from the chart.
If fewer than three in ten of those sentences have data, Task Achievement cannot go
above Band 5.

The shape is claim plus number. The claim says what happened or how two things
compare: rose, fell, was the largest, was twice as high. The number proves it.

A line graph shows visitors to three museums in a European city from 2010 to 2020.
"The Science Museum became much more popular" is a claim with no proof. "The Science
Museum rose from four hundred and twenty thousand visitors in 2010 to seven hundred
and sixty thousand in 2020" is a claim with evidence.

The number must also be true. A table shows teachers' pay in five countries in 2022.
Korean teachers started on thirty-three thousand dollars, Japanese teachers on
thirty-one thousand. "Korean teachers started on thirty-one thousand dollars,
slightly less than Japanese teachers" swaps the rows. A wrong figure is inaccurate
content. One small slip in a detail still allows Band 7; a wrong main trend limits
Task Achievement to Band 6.

Not every number needs to appear. Three to five well-chosen figures per paragraph,
each tied to a claim, beat ten numbers in a row.

================================================================
2. THE RIGHT PREPOSITION WITH EVERY FIGURE (about 2 minutes)
================================================================

This is one of the most common grammar errors in Task 1, and it changes the facts.

"To" gives the end point. "By" gives the size of the change. Two pie charts show how
one country made its electricity: in 2000 coal was forty-eight percent, in 2020
eighteen percent. "Coal fell to eighteen percent" is true. "Coal fell by thirty
percentage points" is also true. "Coal fell by eighteen percent" is false; eighteen
was where it ended, not how far it dropped.

Percent and percentage points are different. Natural gas went from twenty-two
percent to thirty percent. That is a rise of eight percentage points, not "eight
percent". The safest pattern avoids the trap completely: from X to Y. "Natural gas
rose from twenty-two percent to thirty percent." Both ends, no risk.

"At" is for one fixed point: peaked at, stood at. A bar chart shows international
tourist arrivals in Thailand from 2015 to 2025, with a high bar in 2019 and a tiny one
in 2020. "Arrivals peaked at thirty-nine point nine million in 2019 before falling to
six point seven million in 2020." At for the peak, to for the end of the fall, in for
the year.

The noun pattern is "a rise in" the thing "of" the size: "There was a rise in the
share of natural gas of eight percentage points." And shares take "accounted for":
"In 2020, wind and solar accounted for twenty-seven percent of electricity."

One more check. A table shows rice production in India: one hundred and twenty-seven
million tonnes in 2000, one hundred and seventy-eight million in 2020. "India's
production rose by one hundred and seventy-eight million tonnes" is wrong. It rose
to one hundred and seventy-eight million, an increase of fifty-one million.

================================================================
3. APPROXIMATION AND UNITS (about 1 minute 30)
================================================================

On a line graph, many values sit between the gridlines, the guide lines across the
chart. A guessed exact figure risks being wrong; no figure at all means no data. The
answer is approximation: around, roughly, about, nearly, almost, just over, just
under. "The Science Museum rose from around four hundred and twenty thousand visitors
in 2010 to just over seven hundred and fifty thousand in 2020." An exact figure that
is off by more than half the gap between two gridlines counts as a factual error. An
approximation word protects accuracy.

The word must point the right way. Nearly, almost and just under mean below. Just
over means above. Around and roughly mean either side. Coal at forty-eight percent is
"almost half", never "just over half". A pie chart of a student's weekly spending has
food and drinks at thirty-eight percent: "just over a third". A bar chart of coffee
gives Finland twenty-one cups per person a week and Thailand five: Finland drank "just
over four times as many cups".

When the chart prints the exact value, on a pie slice or in a table cell, that value
is given exactly. The approximation can sit beside it: "Coal made up almost half of
electricity in 2000, at forty-eight percent."

Finally, number or amount. Things that can be counted take "the number of": visitors,
hours, cups, households. Things that cannot take "the amount of": electricity, money,
rice. "The amount of visitors" is an error. And the unit always travels with the
figure: seven hundred and sixty thousand visitors, fifty-four hours a week, one
hundred and seventy-eight million tonnes.

================================================================
THE THING WORTH REMEMBERING
================================================================

Claim plus number, in every detail sentence. To for the end, by for the size, at for
a fixed point, and from X to Y when in doubt. Between gridlines, an honest "around" is
better than a precise mistake.
